# 11. Search CD Archives and Historical Software Collections

Magazine cover CDs, shareware compilations and other historical software collections are valuable sources of Quake modifications that may no longer be available online.

During the 1990s and early 2000s, thousands of modifications, maps, patches and utilities were redistributed on physical media. Many of these discs have since been digitised and preserved in online archives.

This chapter explains how to find these collections, search their contents and recover missing releases.

## Understand the Different Types of CD Collections

Not all software CDs were produced in the same way. Understanding the different types of collections helps you identify where a modification might have been distributed.

### Magazine cover CDs

Computer and gaming magazines frequently included CDs containing demos, patches, utilities, maps and modifications. A magazine might promote a particular game in an issue and include a collection of related downloads on its cover disc.

For Quake, these discs could contain:

- New maps and map collections.
- Total conversions and gameplay modifications.
- Bots and other gameplay enhancements.
- Patches and updates.
- Editors and development tools.
- Utilities, skins and other custom content.

The CD might have been assembled by magazine staff, a publisher or a third-party supplier. Its contents could include files collected from websites, FTP servers and community contributors.

Magazine CDs are especially useful because they sometimes preserve material that was only briefly available online.

### Shovelware and shareware compilations

Another source is the large number of software compilations distributed on CD-ROM.

These collections often contained hundreds or thousands of files gathered from public download sites, FTP servers, bulletin board systems (BBSs) and other sources.

Examples include collections associated with sites such as CD-ROM.COM, as well as compilations of BBS software and shareware.

The quality and organisation of these discs varied considerably. Some were carefully categorised, while others were simply large collections of ZIP files grouped into directories.

A Quake modification might be included in a general games collection without any reference to its original project website.

### Semi-commercial collections of third-party material

Some commercial and semi-commercial publishers, particularly in Europe, sold collections of third-party software. Unlike magazine cover discs, these products were often standalone compilations of freeware, shareware and game modifications, sometimes including obscure projects overlooked by larger publishers.

### Other physical media

Although CD-ROMs are the focus here, consider floppy disks, BBS collections and personal backup media. These may contain files that never appeared in commercial compilations or online archives.

## Important CD-ROM Archives

Several online resources make it possible to investigate historical CD-ROM collections without having the original discs.

### Internet Archive

The [Internet Archive](https://archive.org) (`https://archive.org`) hosts a large collection of historical software and CD-ROM images. Its collections include magazine cover discs, shareware compilations and other software collections.

Start by searching the Internet Archive for terms such as:

```
Quake CD
Quake addons
Quake levels
Quake magazine CD
Quake shareware
CD-ROM collection
cover CD
```

You can also search for magazine names, publishers and known CD titles.

When you find a relevant item, inspect its description and available files. A CD-ROM image is commonly provided as an ISO file, although some collections are available as ZIP files or other formats.

If an ISO is available, you can download it and inspect its directory structure on your computer. This lets you browse the disc as if you had the original physical media.

Sometimes the Internet Archive offers a link to inspect the contents of an uploaded CD image without having to download it. Get to know the web interface for the Internet Archive well.

Do not limit your search to discs that mention Quake in their titles. A general magazine cover disc or shareware collection might contain the modification you are looking for.

The archive's search and collection structure can change over time. Search broadly and follow relevant collection links rather than assuming that one search query will find everything.

### QuakeDiscs

[QuakeDiscs](https://QuakeDiscs.com) (https://QuakeDiscs.com) is a catalogue I created for fellow Quake archivists. It indexes third-party add-on CDs for the original Quake, including files nested inside ZIP, RAR, ARJ, PAK and other archives.

You can search for filenames without knowing which disc contains them, then inspect the disc's title, publisher, publication date and directory structure.

For example, you might search for:

```
aq2.zip
actionquake
readme.txt
```

If a matching file is found, inspect the disc's dedicated page. You may be able to identify:

- The CD title.
- Its publisher.
- Its publication date.
- The directory containing the file.
- The archive containing the file, if applicable.
- Other files distributed alongside it.

QuakeDiscs is focused on Quake add-on CDs, so it should not be treated as a complete index of every magazine or general-purpose software disc. Its catalogue also does not directly host the CD images or downloadable files. Instead, it helps you to identify where content was distributed and points towards other sources where discs may be available.


### DiscMaster

[DiscMaster](https://discmaster.textfiles.com) (https://discmaster.textfiles.com) searches filenames and indexed content from historical CD-ROM and floppy disk collections preserved by the Internet Archive. It can identify matching files without requiring you to know which disc contains them.

This makes it a good resource for searching for:

- Modification names.
- Release filenames.
- Readme filenames.
- Unique phrases from documentation.
- Filenames of maps, models, sounds and other custom assets.

For example, suppose you are looking for a modification called *Shadow Arena*. Searching for the project name might not produce a result because the CD's contents are stored under a generic filename. Searching for a distinctive sentence from its readme might locate the file and identify the disc.

DiscMaster can also help you find files that have been extracted from their original ZIP archives. This is important because some CD collections contain the contents of a modification as ordinary files in directories rather than as the original downloadable archive.

### Other historical software collections

Continue looking for other archives and catalogues of historical software. Some specialise in particular platforms, magazine collections, countries or types of physical media.

For example, personal catalogues of magazine cover discs can identify titles that are available on the Internet Archive, while other preservation projects document the contents of particular CD collections.

A useful approach is to search for the names of magazines, publishers and CD collection brands you discover during your research. A disc that is missing from one archive might be preserved elsewhere.

The important lesson is that there is no single complete catalogue of historical software CDs. Treat each resource as another way to discover possible distributions.

## Search for the Modification's Name and Filenames

Search the identified CD archives using project names, release filenames and distinctive content from recovered files.

### Search the modification's name

Search the project's official name, alternative spellings, abbreviations and earlier titles, as described in previous chapters.

A CD might include a modification under a directory named after its author rather than the project. A collection might use a shortened name or a generic category such as `mods`, `addons` or `quake2`.

### Search known release filenames

Search the original filenames of recovered releases and any variations discovered during your research. CD publishers sometimes renamed archives, removed version numbers or shortened filenames when assembling collections.

A match can identify the disc containing the release and its location within the disc's directory structure.

Do not assume that the same release will always have the same filename. A magazine or CD publisher might rename files when assembling a collection.

Try variations of the filename, including shortened names and names without version numbers.

### Search unique filenames inside the modification

When an archive has been renamed or extracted before being added to a disc, its internal filenames may be more useful search terms than the original ZIP filename.

For example:

```
action_readme.txt
aq2_server.cfg
shadow_arena_map.bsp
```

Search for these internal filenames individually.

This technique is particularly useful when:

- The CD publisher renamed the original archive.
- The modification was extracted before being added to the CD.
- The files were placed in a generic directory.
- The project name does not appear in the CD's catalogue.
- The original archive filename is unknown.

### Search distinctive phrases from readme files

Full-text indexes such as DiscMaster may also reveal copies of a modification through distinctive phrases from its readme, credits or installation instructions.

For example, imagine a readme contains this fictional sentence:

```
Shadow Arena introduces a new team-based combat system
with custom weapons and modified player movement.
```

Search for a distinctive part of the sentence, rather than the entire paragraph.

This can locate a copy of the readme even if the modification itself has been renamed or extracted into a directory.

When searching full text, remember that punctuation, spacing and line breaks can vary. Try shorter phrases if an exact search does not produce results.

## Search for Content, Not Just the Original Archive

CD publishers sometimes extracted modifications from their original ZIP archives and placed the contents directly onto the disc. Consequently, the original archive filename may no longer exist in the collection.

For example, the original download might have looked like this:

```
aq2.zip
    readme.txt
    pak0.pak
    pak1.pak
    config.cfg
    maps/
    sound/
```

On a CD, the same modification might appear as:

```
QUAKE2/
    MODS/
        ACTION/
            readme.txt
            pak0.pak
            pak1.pak
            config.cfg
            maps/
            sound/
```

It means you should not dismiss a CD search result just because its file structure differs from the release you have already recovered.

### Compare the contents

When you find a possible copy, compare its contents with your known release.

Look for:

- Matching readme files.
- Matching PAK files.
- Matching maps and other assets.
- Matching configuration files.
- Matching version information.
- Matching file sizes and hashes.

If the files are identical, you have evidence that the CD contains the same release. If some files differ, investigate whether the CD contains a modified copy, a repackaged release or a different version.

A CD publisher might also have included additional documentation, installers or promotional material. Record these items rather than assuming they were part of the original release.

## Investigate the CD and Its Contents

Finding a matching filename is a lead. The next step is to identify the disc and inspect its contents.

If a catalogue such as QuakeDiscs provides a file listing, use it to identify the relevant disc and directory. If you find a disc image on the Internet Archive, download it and inspect its contents.

For an ISO image, you can generally mount it or open it using software that supports ISO files. On many operating systems, mounting an ISO makes its contents accessible as a normal directory.

Once you have access to the disc, inspect the matching files and their surrounding directories. Compare readmes and other contents with known releases, and look for nearby patches, maps or supplementary files.

Also inspect nearby directories. The disc might contain other versions, patches, maps or supplementary files related to the same modification.

Some shareware compilations and historical BBS collections also contain special files that catalogue their downloads or describe individual ZIP archives. These can provide useful clues about original filenames, release descriptions and historical distribution dates.

We will explore these BBS file listings and descriptions, including `ALLFILES.TXT`, `FILES.BBS` and `FILE_ID.DIZ`, in Chapter 21, *Advanced Research Tips*.

### Record the original CD structure

Record the original directory path of each recovered file within the CD. This provides provenance, distinguishes the disc's copy from independently recovered downloads and makes it easier to locate the material again.

This provides useful provenance. It shows where the files were located within the original collection and helps distinguish the CD copy from an independently recovered download.

If you extract files for your own archive, keep the original CD image or source collection separate from your working copy where possible.

Record the CD title, publisher, publication date, source URL and relevant directory path in your research notes.

## Distinguish Release Dates from CD Distribution Dates

A modification might have been released online in March 1998 but included on a magazine CD published in September. The CD's publication date provides evidence of later distribution, not necessarily the original release date.

### Be careful with file timestamps

File timestamps may have been preserved or changed as files were packaged, copied onto CDs and later extracted. A timestamp inside a ZIP archive may differ from the timestamp of the extracted file. Treat these as supporting evidence rather than definitive release dates.

### Record the different dates separately

When you discover a release on a CD, record both the release information and the CD distribution information.

For example, an entry in `research/history.md` might look like:

```
#### Version 1.5

- Release date: March 1998 (estimated)
- Original filename: action15.zip
- Recovered from: PC Zone Complete Quake CD
- CD publisher: PC Zone Magazine
- CD publication date: September 1998
- CD directory: QUAKE/MODS/ACTION/
- CD source: [Internet Archive URL]
- Files: readme.txt, pak0.pak, pak1.pak
- SHA-256: ...
```

This example records the recovered files alongside their physical distribution source.

If the dates appear inconsistent, investigate further. Perhaps the release date is wrong, the CD date refers to a different edition, or the files on the disc were added after its original publication. Do not silently resolve the discrepancy by choosing one date.


## Repackage Recovered Releases Carefully

When a modification survives only as extracted files on a CD, you may want to create a new ZIP archive to make the release easier to distribute.

This is reasonable, provided the reconstructed archive is clearly distinguished from the original download.

To make the recovered release easier to distribute and use, you may want to repackage these files into a ZIP archive.

This is reasonable, but it is important to distinguish a repackaged archival copy from the original release file.

### Use a `_repack` suffix

When creating a new archive from files recovered in an extracted or otherwise modified form, use a filename that clearly identifies it as a repackaged copy.

For example:

```
shadowarena11_repack.zip
```

Do not give the new archive the original filename as though it were the original download. Someone retrieving the file should be able to tell that it has been repackaged by the archivist.

If the original archive is recovered elsewhere, preserve it separately under its original filename.

### Preserve the recovered contents

When repackaging, avoid changing the contents of the release unnecessarily.

- Preserve the original filenames.
- Preserve the directory structure where possible.
- Do not modify readme files or configuration files.
- Do not add new documentation inside the repackaged archive unless there is a clear reason.
- Do not silently remove files that appear unnecessary.
- Record any changes you make to the recovered contents.

The purpose is to make the recovered files accessible, not to create a modified version of the original release.

If you need to repair, rename or otherwise alter files, document those changes separately.

### Record the repackaging in `history.md`

The release entry in `research/history.md` should explain that the original archive was not recovered and that the downloadable copy was created from files found on a CD-ROM.

For example:

```
#### Version 1.1

- Original filename: shadowarena11.zip
- Recovered from: PC Zone Complete Quake CD
- CD directory: QUAKE/MODS/SHADOW/
- Original archive recovered: No
- Archival copy: shadowarena11_repack.zip
- Repackaging: Created from extracted files on the CD-ROM
- Contents: Preserved from the CD directory
- SHA-256: ...
```

Also record the source CD and its publication date, as described earlier in this chapter.

### Why this distinction matters

Repackaging creates a new archival object, even when the recovered files themselves remain unchanged. A future researcher should be able to distinguish between an original distribution and an archive reconstructed from its contents.

**Preserve what you find, make recovered content accessible when necessary, and document anything you reconstruct or repackage.**

## Summary

Historical CD-ROM collections can preserve Quake modifications that have disappeared from online sources. Resources such as the Internet Archive, QuakeDiscs and DiscMaster help identify discs and search their contents.

Search for release filenames, distinctive internal files and phrases from documentation. Once you locate a possible copy, inspect its contents and preserve the details of its original distribution.

When recovering files from CD-ROMs, distinguish original releases from repackaged copies and document the source of each recovered file.

**Outcome:** You can identify releases preserved on physical media and recover files that might not exist on the original project website.
