# **How to create a Quake modification archive**

We now understand why Quake modifications are worth preserving and what a good modification archive should contain.

It is time to put that knowledge into practice.

Creating an archive involves several different activities. You need to choose a subject, establish a structure for recording information, research the modification's history, locate and recover original files, verify your discoveries and publish the results.

This is a form of digital archaeology. You are investigating a fragmented digital past, connecting surviving pieces of information and recovering files that might otherwise be lost.

The process is not always straightforward. Websites have disappeared, download links are broken, files have been renamed and historical records are incomplete. You will often need to follow clues from one source to another, revisiting earlier discoveries as new information becomes available.

In this section, we'll work through the practical process of creating an archive, from choosing a topic to publishing a finished resource.

The material is organised into three parts:

1. Establish the archive
2. Research and recover
3. Verify and publish

Let's dig deeper.

## **Part 1. Establish the archive**

Before beginning substantial research, establish what you are archiving and how you will organise the results.

These first chapters help you define the project, create a working structure and establish a system for recording discoveries.

### **Chapter 1. Choose an archive topic**

Learn how to select a suitable archival subject and define its scope.

Topics might include a specific modification, a category of modifications, a particular developer or a historical collection.

We'll also consider the difference between a small, focused archive and a comprehensive long-term preservation project.

**Outcome:** You have selected a topic and defined what your archive will cover.

### **Chapter 2. Design the archive structure**

Learn how to organise your archive so that information, evidence and recovered files can be stored systematically.

We'll establish a directory structure and introduce the Markdown documents used to record the project's history, releases, files and references.

**Outcome:** You have a working directory and a basic structure for your archive.

### **Chapter 3. Establish the archival record**

Learn how to turn research discoveries into structured historical information.

We'll establish documents for recording the project chronology, known releases, files, historical download locations, project homepages and other references.

We'll also cover preserving evidence and recording uncertainty so that historical claims can be traced back to their sources.

**Outcome:** You have a system for recording discoveries as you research.

## **Part 2. Research and recover**

This is the largest part of the guide.

The objective is to discover what was released, when it was released, where it was distributed and which original files can still be recovered.

Historical information is scattered across many different sources. Each source has its own strengths, and using several sources together is often necessary to reconstruct a project's history.

The following chapters introduce the major sources and techniques.

### **Chapter 4. Research original project websites**

Learn how to find and investigate original modification homepages and developer websites.

We'll cover the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, archived webpages, project news pages, historical download links, changelogs, developer information and related projects.

**Outcome:** You can extract historical release information and identify original distribution locations from surviving project websites.

### **Chapter 5. Research gaming news archives**

Learn how to search contemporary gaming news websites for announcements and references to modifications.

We'll investigate resources such as Blue's News, The Redwoods / Stomped, Shacknews and Quake2.com.

We'll cover searching news archives by project name, developer, filename and known release date.

**Outcome:** You can use contemporary news reporting to discover releases, project websites, download links and historical evidence.

### **Chapter 6. Search Usenet archives**

Learn how to use surviving Usenet archives to find historical discussions and announcements.

We'll cover searching for modification names, filenames, developers, alternative spellings and distinctive content from recovered files.

**Outcome:** You can use Usenet to discover release announcements, developer information and additional historical references.

### **Chapter 7. Search CD archives and historical software collections**

Learn how to investigate magazine CDs and other physical media that distributed Quake modifications.

We'll explore the Internet Archive, QuakeDiscs.com and DiscMaster at textfiles.com.

We'll also cover searching by unique filenames and distinctive readme content, which can reveal files distributed under unfamiliar names.

**Outcome:** You can identify and recover releases preserved on historical CD-ROMs.

### **Chapter 8. Search the modern web and search engines**

Learn how to use modern search engines to locate surviving references and copies of historical files.

We'll cover Google, Yandex, DuckDuckGo and other search engines, including techniques for searching exact filenames, project names, developer names and unique content.

**Outcome:** You can systematically search the modern web using information accumulated during your research.

### **Chapter 9. Search FTP archives and file indexes**

Learn how to locate surviving files on historical FTP servers and through FTP search services.

We'll investigate resources such as FileMood and MMNT, along with techniques for searching directory listings, identifying mirrors and locating alternative copies of releases.

**Outcome:** You can find surviving files and historical directory listings across FTP archives.

### **Chapter 10. Search other Quake archives and communities**

Learn how to investigate existing Quake file collections and connect with other people interested in preservation.

We'll explore resources such as Quaddicted, Shub-Hub, Gamers.org archives and collections maintained by individual enthusiasts.

We'll also discuss how to approach other archivists, request searches of their collections and build relationships within the Quake preservation community.

**Outcome:** You can extend your research beyond publicly indexed websites and discover files held by other collectors.

### **Chapter 11. Investigate historical mirrors**

Learn how to identify alternative locations where original project websites and downloads might have been preserved.

We'll investigate PlanetQuake mirrors, FTP mirrors, Gamers.org directory structures and other historical distribution locations.

We'll also cover techniques for identifying alternative hostnames and directory paths based on known hosting arrangements.

**Outcome:** You can systematically investigate historical mirrors and locate files that are missing from their original hosting locations.

## **Part 3. Verify and publish**

Having researched the project and recovered files, it is time to turn the discoveries into a reliable public archive.

These final chapters focus on improving research efficiency, verifying recovered material and publishing and maintaining the finished resource.

### **Chapter 12. Verify, classify and connect discoveries**

Learn how to assess recovered files and connect them to the correct releases and historical evidence.

We'll cover identifying duplicates, comparing different versions, checking archive contents, recording provenance, resolving conflicting information and documenting uncertainty.

**Outcome:** You can turn a collection of recovered files and historical references into a coherent and defensible archival record.

### **Chapter 13. Publish the archive**

Learn how to turn your working archive into a publicly accessible resource.

We'll cover preparing documentation, publishing Markdown files, organising downloadable files, creating navigable indexes and using GitHub or another suitable hosting platform.

We'll also discuss practical considerations such as storage limits, hosting costs, copyright and redistribution permissions.

**Outcome:** You have published an archive that other people can browse, research and use.

### **Chapter 14. Maintain and improve the archive**

Learn how to maintain an archive as an ongoing preservation project.

We'll cover accepting contributions, adding newly discovered releases, updating historical information, correcting errors, preserving additional evidence and maintaining backups.

**Outcome:** You understand how to keep an archive useful and improve its completeness over time.

## **Putting it all together**

The above parts and chapters take you through the complete archival process.

One important thing to remember is that archival research is iterative.

You might find a release announcement that leads you to a filename, which leads you to a magazine CD, which reveals an earlier version, which leads you to a developer's personal website.

Each discovery creates new opportunities for research.

You should therefore expect to move between chapters and revisit earlier work as new information becomes available. The directory structure and archival records established in Part 1 provide a place to capture these discoveries as they happen.

You also don't need to complete every chapter to produce something valuable. A small archive documenting one modification and preserving a single recovered release is already a meaningful contribution.

**Remember:** The ultimate goal is to turn scattered files and fragmented historical information into an organised, documented and publicly accessible record of Quake modding history.
