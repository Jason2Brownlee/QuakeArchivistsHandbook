# 4. How to Create a Quake Modification Archive

We now understand why Quake modifications are worth preserving and what a good modification archive should contain.

It is time to put that knowledge into practice.

Creating an archive involves several activities, from choosing a subject and researching its history to recovering original files, documenting discoveries and publishing the results.

This is a form of digital archaeology. You are investigating a fragmented digital past, following clues, connecting surviving pieces of information and recovering files that might otherwise be lost.

The process is rarely straightforward. Websites have disappeared, download links are broken, files have been renamed and historical records are incomplete. One discovery often leads to another, and you may need to revisit earlier research as new information comes to light.

In this section, we'll work through the practical process of creating a Quake modification archive.

The material is organised into three parts:

1. Establish the archive
2. Research and recover
3. Verify and publish

## Part 1. Establish the Archive

Before beginning substantial research, you need to decide what you are archiving and establish a place to organise your discoveries.

We'll look at how to choose a suitable project, define its scope and create a simple working directory for files, documentation and research.

We'll also establish the historical records that will form the foundation of your archive, including release information, historical download locations, references and outstanding questions.

The aim is to create a lightweight structure that makes it easy to begin researching immediately and keeps your findings organised as the project grows.

## Part 2. Research and Recover

This is the largest part of the handbook and, for me, the most exciting.

The goal is to reconstruct the history of a modification and recover as many of its original files as possible.

We'll explore the many places where evidence and files might survive, including historical project websites, gaming news archives, Usenet, magazine CDs, FTP servers, file mirrors and existing Quake collections.

We'll also look at using modern search engines, investigating forgotten distribution locations, reaching out to original developers, collectors and other archivists, and building small tools to automate repetitive research tasks.

Along the way, we'll cover practical techniques for following historical clues, identifying missing releases, finding alternative download locations and searching collections that ordinary search engines may overlook.

This is where much of the treasure hunting happens. A single filename, old announcement or broken download link can lead to an unexpected discovery.

## Part 3. Verify and Publish

Finding files is only part of creating a useful archive.

You also need to understand what you have recovered, establish where it came from and connect it to the modification's history.

We'll look at how to compare releases, identify duplicates, investigate conflicting information and document the evidence supporting your conclusions.

We'll then turn your findings into a public archive that others can browse, download from and contribute to.

Finally, we'll consider how to maintain and improve the archive over time as new files, information and contributions become available.

The aim is to turn your research into a reliable and accessible resource that can continue to preserve Quake history for years to come.

## Putting It All Together

Although the handbook is presented as a sequence of steps, archival research is rarely linear.

You might discover a release announcement that reveals an original filename. That filename leads you to a magazine CD, which contains an unknown version, which leads you to a developer's personal website.

Each discovery can open new directions for research.

You should expect to move between activities, revisit previous findings and update your understanding as you learn more.

You also do not need to complete every step before publishing something useful. Even a small archive documenting one modification and preserving a handful of files can make a valuable contribution.

**The goal is simple: find what survives, document what you discover and make it available so that another piece of Quake history is not lost.**
