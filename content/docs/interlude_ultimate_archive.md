# Interlude: The Siren Song of the Ultimate Quake Archive

After creating a few Quake archives, something interesting starts to happen.

You begin discovering enormous collections of Quake files that aren't easily accessible to the public.

Perhaps it's an old FTP directory preserved in the Wayback Machine, a collection of magazine CDs on the Internet Archive, or a personal backup containing thousands of files.

You start to realise just how much Quake material is still out there, scattered across the internet.

And then a dangerous thought occurs.

**Why not just archive everything?**

It happens to me regularly.

I'll discover another enormous collection of files and start thinking that perhaps I should stop creating these small, focused archives and instead build one enormous archive containing every Quake file I can find.

One archive to rule them all.

I'm not alone in this.

Over the years, several people have contacted me about archiving Quake modifications and suggested essentially the same thing.

Why not preserve everything?

I've also encountered other enthusiasts with ambitions to create the ultimate Quake archive, collecting every modification, map, tool, patch, and release ever made.

It's an exciting idea.

But I haven't personally seen any of these ambitious projects emerge as complete, publicly accessible archives.

And I think I understand why.

## The Trap

The problem is that an archive of everything is an archive that never ends.

There are potentially terabytes of files to collect, store, and serve. There are duplicates, renamed files, different versions, incomplete releases, and countless unrelated projects.

How do you organise everything?

How do you make it searchable?

How do you distinguish between original releases, mirrors, modified copies, and duplicate files?

And when can you ever consider the project finished?

I could handle that, it might even be fun, but...

How much will it cost to host and maintain? Forever?

Some of the most successful Quake preservation resources, such as Quaddicted, Shub-Hub, and Quaketastic, have taken more practical approaches, making large collections of Quake files accessible through useful categories, collections, and areas of interest.

They provide enormous value without requiring someone to reconstruct the complete history of every Quake file ever created.

I sometimes think of the ultimate Quake archive as an *attractor* in a dynamical system, or perhaps a black hole.

The longer you spend archiving Quake, the more it draws you in.

Eventually, almost everyone seems to arrive at the same idea.

Wouldn't it be wonderful if we could just preserve everything?

Yes, it would.

But it's a trap.

## Resist the Temptation

My advice is to resist the siren song of the ultimate Quake archive.

The danger is not collecting widely. It is allowing an enormous, ever-growing collection to become a prerequisite for publishing anything useful.

There is no reason that preserving Quake history must be an all-or-nothing undertaking. Small, independent archives can coexist with larger preservation projects, and both approaches can contribute to the same goal.

Perhaps one day we'll work out a practical way to build the ultimate Quake file archive: comprehensive, searchable, inexpensive to host, and easy for anyone to contribute to.

I'd love to see it.

For now, we have some remarkable large collections on cloud storage and FTP servers, alongside many smaller, focused archival projects.

And perhaps that's not such a bad arrangement.

**I'd rather see a hundred small, useful archives made public than one enormous archive that never gets finished.**
